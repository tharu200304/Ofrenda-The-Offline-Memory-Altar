# Mateo Ramos (1928 – 2015)
## Workshop Notes and Stories from "El Cedro Dorado" (Coyoacán)

### 1. The Soul of Wood and Classical Guitars
Wood never truly dies; it merely stops breathing air and begins breathing music. In my workshop on Calle Francisco Sosa in Coyoacán, I worked with Mexican cypress, jacaranda, and red cedar. When building a guitar top, you must tap the spruce with your knuckles until it rings with a clear tenor tap tone. 
A good guitar takes forty days to assemble and forty years to mature. When you strum a chord on a fifty-year-old instrument, you are not merely vibrating steel and nylon—you are hearing the rain and thunder that fell on the tree when it was still a sapling on the hillsides of Michoacán.

### 2. The Great Earthquake of September 1985
On the morning of September 19, 1985, the earth shook as if the roots of the valley were tearing apart. Our house held firm, thanks to the thick adobe and heavy cedar joists, but the apartment blocks down the avenue collapsed into clouds of gray plaster. For three weeks, we turned our workshop into a community soup kitchen. Elena baked bread day and night, and I used all my spare cedar planks to construct sturdy stretcher frames and long dining tables for the displaced families. We learned then that a family is not defined by the walls of your own bedroom, but by who you are willing to shelter when the earth shivers.

### 3. A Father's Advice on Patience and Craft
To my grandchildren: In your modern world, you believe that because a telephone can send a message in three seconds, life itself should move at the speed of a spark. It is a lie. Everything truly worth keeping takes time to season. 
- A good cut of cedar must dry in the shade for seven seasons before a chisel touches it.
- A lasting marriage requires seven thousand shared cups of black coffee and forgiveness before sunrise.
- A true craftsman does not rush the final polish; the finest sheen comes from the friction of your own palm.
When you rush, you leave no room for your heart to enter the work.

### 4. Elena's Wooden Box
The proudest piece I ever carved was not a concert guitar sold to a maestro in Bellas Artes. It was a small 5-inch jewel box made from a single scrap of fragrant red cedar in October 1951. I carved a single relief of a hummingbird hovering over a five-petaled flower on the lid. Inside, I placed a dried cempasúchil blossom. When I handed it to Elena in Alameda Central, my hands were shaking so hard I nearly dropped it on the cobblestones. She smiled, touched my fingers, and held that little box on her nightstand for sixty-four years.
